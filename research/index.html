My research

I study the brief flashes of light produced by stellar explosions and compact binary mergers. By comparing when a source appears in X-rays and how it brightens or fades in optical images, I look for clues to what produced it.

An X-ray counterpart to a short gamma-ray burst

My collaborators and I searched Einstein Probe observations taken around the times of known short gamma-ray bursts. We found about eight seconds of soft X-ray emission from EP260411a coincident with GRB 260411A. The pulse peaked later and lasted longer at lower photon energies. The timing and spectra support a common origin for the two signals, and the burst properties are consistent with a compact object merger. Read the preprint.

Optical counterparts to fast X-ray transients

When Einstein Probe detects an X-ray flash, optical survey archives can tell us what happened at the same position in the days that followed. My collaborators and I used this approach to associate EP240506a with AT 2024ofs. The optical transient appeared on the outskirts of a galaxy at redshift 0.12, and its brightness and evolution are consistent with a core-collapse supernova. Read the ApJ paper.

Finding kilonovae in wide-field surveys

A gravitational-wave alert can leave hundreds of optical candidates to follow up. Kilonovae fade quickly, so deciding where to point a telescope matters in the first few nights.

I developed a temporal convolutional network that uses three nights of multiband photometry together with a distance estimate from the gravitational-wave signal. In simulated Wide Field Survey Telescope observations, our best model achieved 95% precision and 94% recall. Read the Universe paper or explore the classifier.
